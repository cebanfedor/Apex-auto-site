Repairing a car after auction: what is realistic and why it is not always expensive

The scary words on a lot page — “Front End”, “Side”, “Frame” — frighten people more than the repair actually costs. Almost everything that happens to a car in a crash can be fixed. The question is not “can it be done” but “how many hours, which parts, and is the saving worth it”. Here we break down what the cost is made of, what is easy, and what is genuinely hard.

Apex Auto editorial team Blog “Parts & repair” Published: November 27, 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways

  • Almost everything can be fixed: body panels, paint, quarter panel replacement, frame rail straightening, airbags, engine and transmission — all of this is routine work for body shops and engine shops.
  • Cost is hours and parts: we count labor, parts prices, paint and a reserve for hidden damage. The same “damaged” car can be cheap or not worth it — it all depends on what exactly is damaged.
  • The most expensive thing is not “major repair” but uncertainty: when hidden damage cannot be seen in the photos, we build in a reserve or pass on the lot.
  • The profit is the difference: the lot price plus shipping and repair must stay noticeably below the price of the same car in good condition.

Why repair after auction seems scary

The insurance company calculates repair at US shop prices and with new original parts. If the repair estimate approaches the value of the car, the insurer writes it off and sells it at auction. That is why a car listed as “damaged” on a lot page may be one that a good body shop restores for a reasonable price: the shop has different labor rates, different sources of parts and different tolerances.

This does not mean every lot is a good deal. It means the repair has to be assessed on its merits: what is damaged, which parts are needed, where the hidden part is, and how long it takes.

What the repair cost is made of

  • Labor. Technicians’ hours: disassembly, replacement, welding, paint, assembly, adjustments. The more parts are welded on, the more hours.
  • Parts. New original, quality aftermarket or used from a dismantler. The price difference between the options can be several times over, and each part has to be sourced separately.
  • Paint. Often the biggest and most underestimated line: preparation, color matching, three-layer and pearlescent finishes, blending into neighboring panels.
  • Diagnostics and geometry. Measuring the body, checking the suspension, scanning the electronics, calibrating sensors and cameras.
  • Hidden damage. What only shows up after disassembly. A good estimate always includes a reserve for it.

What is easy and what is hard

This is a guide to difficulty, not a price list: actual amounts depend on make, year and region.

How we assess a lot before bidding

  1. We look at all the lot photos, the damage assessment and the auction’s repair estimate, but we do our own calculation: we do not take those figures “as is”.
  2. We separate the damage into what is visible and what is likely hidden behind it: for example, behind a deformed bumper there is often a reinforcement bar and the radiator.
  3. We add up labor, parts and paint, and add a reserve for the hidden part.
  4. We compare the total of “lot plus shipping plus customs clearance plus repair” with the price of a comparable car in good condition.
  5. If the profit disappears or too much uncertainty remains, we say so honestly: it is better to take a different lot.

When it is better to walk away from a lot

  • damage to the load-bearing structure in several places at once: frame rail, pillar and rocker panel together;
  • the photos do not show the extent of the damage, and the reserve for hidden damage exceeds the possible saving;
  • rare, expensive parts are needed that are not on sale or at dismantlers;
  • salt-water flooding or a severe fire in the cabin — the electrics and corrosion cause hidden, long-term problems;
  • the repair cost together with shipping and customs clearance approaches the price of the same car in good condition.

FAQ

Is it true that repairing a damaged car always costs as much as buying a new one?
No. That is only true in severe cases, for example serious structural damage together with electrical damage. For most lots where the exterior panels, bumpers, suspension or one or two parts of the body are damaged, the repair is a noticeably smaller share of the car’s value than in the US estimate.
Can I find out the repair cost before buying?
You can estimate it from the photos and the damage description, but it is just that — an estimate with a reserve for hidden damage. It becomes more accurate after inspection and disassembly. That is why an estimate always includes a reserve, and when in doubt the lot is not bought.
What costs more: paint or replacing the part?
It depends on the part. For bolt-on panels the part often costs less than good paint; for welded ones, part of the cost is welding and anti-corrosion work. That is why the estimate is calculated line by line.
How do I check the quality of the repair after buying?
Ask for the body geometry measurement report from before and after the repair, photos of the work stages, a list of replaced parts and the electronics scan results. A good shop provides these documents without any questions.
